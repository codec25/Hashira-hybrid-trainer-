# HASHIRA Knowledge Pack — Squat Family

Status: researched canonical family model. Documentation only; not executable programming logic yet.

## 1. Canonical identity

**Family:** Squat / bilateral knee-dominant lower-body pattern  
**Primary patterns:** squat, knee extension, hip extension, ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion coordination  
**Common disciplines:** calisthenics, gym/resistance training, Pilates-adjacent control work, mobility, athletic preparation  
**Not equivalent to:** hinge, lunge/split squat, jump, wall sit

A squat is not one fixed technique. Stance width, foot angle, trunk angle, shank angle, depth, external load, bar placement, tempo, assistance, and heel elevation all change the mechanical demands. HASHIRA must therefore model squat *variants* rather than pretend one visual template is universally correct.

## 2. Evidence basis

Research used for this family includes:

- Currier BS et al. ACSM Position Stand: Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults. Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2026;58(4):851-872. PMID 41843416.
- Lorenzetti S et al. How to squat? Effects of various stance widths, foot placement angles and level of experience on knee, hip and trunk motion and loading. BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil. 2018. PMID 30026952.
- Hopkins JE, Hopkins CE, Chiu LZF. Greater squat stance width alters three-dimensional hip moment demands. J Biomech. 2024;177:112391. PMID 39486379.
- Graber KA et al. The effect of trunk and shank position on the hip-to-knee moment ratio in a bilateral squat. Phys Ther Sport. 2023;61:49-56. PMID 37001335.
- Almosnino S et al. Three-dimensional knee joint moments during performance of the bodyweight squat: effects of stance width and foot rotation. J Appl Biomech. 2013;29(1):33-43. PMID 23462440.
- Kubo K et al. Effects of squat training with different depths on lower limb muscle volumes. Eur J Appl Physiol. 2019. PMID 31230110.
- Martínez-Cava A et al. Full squat produces greater neuromuscular and functional adaptations and lower pain than partial squats after prolonged resistance training. Eur J Sport Sci. 2020;20(1):115-124. PMID 31092132.
- Bazyler CD et al. The efficacy of incorporating partial squats in maximal strength training. J Strength Cond Res. 2014;28(11):3024-3032. PMID 24662234.

Evidence is strongest for healthy adults and resistance-training contexts. HASHIRA should not universalize findings to rehabilitation, pathology, pregnancy, or post-operative populations without additional domain-specific evidence.

## 3. What the squat trains

Depending on execution and loading, the squat family can train:

- knee-extensor strength and hypertrophy
- hip-extensor strength and hypertrophy
- lower-body muscular endurance
- trunk force transfer and bracing
- ankle/knee/hip coordination
- usable lower-body range of motion
- balance and positional control
- power when paired with appropriate intent/loading or progressed to jump patterns

The family should not be described as "a quad exercise" only. Joint moments shift with technique. More forward trunk lean tends to increase the hip-to-knee moment ratio; greater forward shank angle tends to shift demand toward the knee extensors. Stance width also changes three-dimensional hip and knee demands.

## 4. Technique is contextual, not one ideal silhouette

HASHIRA must avoid simplistic rules such as:

- "knees must never pass toes"
- "feet must always point straight"
- "everyone must use shoulder-width stance"
- "deeper is always better"
- "partial squats are always wrong"
- "torso must stay perfectly vertical"

Instead, technique should be judged against:

1. the selected squat variant
2. the athlete's anthropometry and available ROM
3. the intended training outcome
4. control and stability
5. symptoms/pain
6. external load and fatigue

Research shows stance width, foot angle, trunk position, and shank position alter joint moments. Extreme combinations can create substantially different loading. Therefore HASHIRA should treat stance and foot angle as configurable characteristics, not universal faults.

## 5. Depth model

Depth is represented as a continuum rather than pass/fail.

Suggested internal categories:

- **box/high partial** — deliberately limited depth
- **partial** — above approximately parallel
- **parallel-region** — thigh near horizontal / context-dependent
- **deep/full available** — athlete reaches a deep controlled position within the selected variant

Research comparing full and partial squat training suggests deeper/full-ROM training often transfers well to strength and lower-body muscle development, while partial squats can still be useful for specific overload, range-specific strength, sport preparation, or staged progression. HASHIRA should therefore prefer useful controlled ROM for general training while allowing intentional partial-ROM programming when the purpose is explicit.

Do not use camera-estimated knee angle alone to declare a rep "invalid" unless the session specifically defines a depth target.

## 6. Canonical progression tree

This is a *capability progression*, not a mandatory ladder. Athletes may enter at different points.

### Entry / assisted
1. Supported sit-to-stand
2. High box squat
3. Assisted squat using rail/TRX/support
4. Controlled bodyweight squat to comfortable depth

### Foundational
5. Bodyweight squat
6. Tempo bodyweight squat
7. Pause squat
8. Deeper bodyweight squat when controlled

### Load progressions
9. Goblet squat
10. Double-dumbbell / kettlebell squat
11. Front-loaded squat variants
12. Barbell back/front squat variants

### Relative-strength / unilateral bridge
13. Split squat family (separate canonical family)
14. Step-up family
15. Assisted single-leg squat patterns
16. Pistol/shrimp family where appropriate

### Power bridge
17. Squat-to-calf-raise / rapid concentric intent
18. Low-amplitude squat jump
19. Jump-squat family

Power and unilateral movements become separate families once their demands dominate.

## 7. Regression logic

If the athlete cannot perform the intended squat with appropriate control, regress by preserving the purpose:

- add external support
- raise target/box height
- reduce depth temporarily
- reduce load
- slow tempo
- use counterbalance
- elevate heels when ankle ROM/setup makes this useful
- choose a more stable load position
- reduce set length/fatigue

A regression is successful if it allows the athlete to practice the desired pattern or adaptation with better control and tolerable symptoms.

## 8. Progression logic

Progression options include:

- greater external load
- greater controlled ROM
- improved technique at same load
- increased reps/sets within programming limits
- reduced assistance
- pause duration
- tempo emphasis
- harder load position
- unilateral bias
- power intent
- increased density for endurance goals

HASHIRA should choose the progression mechanism that matches the goal. A hypertrophy athlete does not automatically need a more acrobatic squat; a calisthenics athlete does not always need external load; a beginner does not need complexity just because they can perform more reps.

## 9. Coaching cues

Cues should be short, contextual, and selected only when useful.

Possible setup cues:

- "Choose a stance that lets you move comfortably and stay balanced."
- "Keep pressure through the whole foot."
- "Brace before you descend."

Possible movement cues:

- "Sit down between your hips."
- "Let knees and hips bend together."
- "Track the knees in the same general direction as the feet."
- "Stay balanced over the mid-foot."
- "Stand tall through the floor."

Do not stack many cues at once. The coach/engine should choose the cue with the highest likely learning value.

## 10. Common observable issues

These are coaching observations, not automatic diagnoses.

- loss of whole-foot pressure / heel or forefoot instability
- asymmetric shift
- unintended collapse into a depth the athlete cannot control
- loss of intended trunk position for the chosen variant
- knees moving in a direction inconsistent with the athlete's chosen stance/foot orientation
- abrupt uncontrolled descent
- failure to reach a session-specific depth target
- inability to stand from the selected load/depth
- technique deterioration from fatigue

Important: "knee valgus" or "trunk lean" should not automatically be labeled dangerous. Camera-based flags must describe observed relationships and confidence rather than diagnose injury risk from a single frame.

## 11. Programming metadata

### General strength
Use a squat variation that permits high-quality progressive resistance. Heavy loading is appropriate only after the athlete has the skill and setup to use it. The ACSM 2026 position stand supports heavier loading for maximal-strength development, but the precise prescription depends on the exercise and athlete.

### Hypertrophy
Squat variants can contribute substantial quadriceps and gluteal training. Dose should be considered together with all other lower-body work. Deeper controlled squatting may increase training stimulus for some lower-body muscles, but HASHIRA should avoid presenting one exact depth as mandatory for every athlete.

### Muscular endurance
Use manageable variants and loading that allow repeated high-quality repetitions without turning technique breakdown into the goal.

### Power
Use a squat pattern/load that allows high movement intent and technical consistency; transition to jump-family exercises when leaving the ground becomes the defining task.

### Mobility/control
Bodyweight, supported, counterbalanced, paused, or tempo squats may be used to explore/control available ankle, knee, hip, and trunk positions. Do not market this as fixing a diagnosed mobility disorder.

## 12. Fatigue model

Suggested relative metadata (to be calibrated later):

- local quadriceps fatigue: moderate-high depending on variant/dose
- glute/hip extensor fatigue: moderate-high depending on trunk/stance/load
- systemic fatigue: low for unloaded practice; potentially high for heavy multi-set barbell work
- coordination demand: low-moderate for basic squat, higher under heavy load or complex variants
- impact: low unless transitioned to jump family
- recovery cost: dose-dependent; never inferred from exercise name alone

The engine must calculate recent exposure from actual session volume/intensity, not mark "legs recovered in 48 hours" as a fixed rule.

## 13. Vision model — future Movement Lab

### Camera-observable landmarks
Future pose tracking can use:

- shoulders
- hips
- knees
- ankles
- heels/feet where model quality permits

### Derived signals
Potentially useful signals:

- hip/knee/ankle vertical displacement
- estimated knee flexion angle
- estimated hip flexion angle
- trunk inclination
- shank inclination
- left/right symmetry
- movement velocity/cadence
- rep phase
- estimated depth relative to the athlete's own calibrated range
- knee direction relative to foot direction when foot landmarks are reliable

### Rep state machine
A conservative future model:

`standing -> descent -> bottom transition -> ascent -> standing`

A rep should only be counted when temporal movement confirms the full state cycle. One frame must never determine a rep.

### Camera limitations
Single-phone video cannot reliably measure internal joint forces, muscle activation, pain, exact load distribution, or injury risk. Depth and joint-angle estimates are sensitive to camera angle, occlusion, clothing, model confidence, and perspective.

HASHIRA should therefore use confidence thresholds and phrases such as "It looks like..." for uncertain coaching observations.

## 14. iOS/native-portability rule

Movement Lab must expose platform-neutral derived signals, e.g.:

```json
{
  "movement_family": "squat",
  "phase": "ascent",
  "rep_count": 7,
  "depth_ratio": 0.88,
  "tempo_seconds": 2.4,
  "symmetry_signal": 0.94,
  "tracking_confidence": 0.91
}
```

The workout/knowledge engine should consume these signals rather than depend directly on browser-specific camera APIs. On web/PWA the capture layer can use browser APIs; a future Capacitor/native iOS implementation can provide the same signal contract from a native camera/vision layer without rewriting programming logic.

## 15. Safety boundaries

Stop/modify guidance may be appropriate when:

- user reports sharp/new/concerning pain
- loss of balance creates immediate fall risk
- external load is clearly uncontrolled
- the athlete explicitly indicates they cannot continue safely

HASHIRA must not diagnose "bad knees," meniscus injury, impingement, spinal injury, or other pathology from squat video.

## 16. Evidence confidence

- Squat kinematics/kinetics vary with stance, foot angle, trunk, shank, depth: **high confidence**
- Progressive resistance using squat variants improves lower-body strength: **high confidence**
- Full/deeper ROM is generally useful for broad strength/hypertrophy development: **moderate-high confidence**, context-dependent
- Partial ROM can have specific programming value: **moderate confidence**
- One universal ideal squat stance: **not supported**
- Camera can diagnose injury risk from ordinary squat video: **not supported**

## 17. HASHIRA decision principle

The Smart Workout Engine should never ask simply:

> "Can this athlete squat?"

It should ask:

> "Which squat-family variant best serves today's goal, given this athlete's capability, recent training, equipment, available time, symptoms, and desired adaptation — and what regression is available if that choice does not work today?"
